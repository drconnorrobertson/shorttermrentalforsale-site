Cost Segregation and Bonus Depreciation for STR Buyers

Published 2026-08-22 · Updated 2026-11-21 · BNB Accelerator Editorial Team

Start with a supported basis, not a percentage of the asking price

A buyer’s first question is not how large a deduction a seller or calculator advertises. It is whether the proposed acquisition has a traceable cost basis, documented assets and a tax treatment that fits the buyer. A listing price, furniture photograph or projected revenue sheet does not provide that file. Keep a purchase model that works without assumed tax savings while the actual tax review is unresolved.

IRS Publication 527 explains that land is not depreciable and that rental depreciation depends on basis, recovery period and method. Depreciation also reduces basis for a later disposition. Do not count the down payment, mortgage principal and purchase basis as interchangeable numbers. Your advisor should reconcile the closing records and identify the appropriate allocation and tax treatment for the actual property and use.

A fictional allocation bridge

Assume, only to illustrate reconciliation, that a buyer pays $500,000 and the reviewed allocation assigns $100,000 to land and $400,000 to depreciable assets. Suppose an asset-level review supports $70,000 of qualifying shorter-lived components within that $400,000, leaving $330,000 allocated to the building. These are invented amounts, not a typical allocation, expected study result or estimate for a real listing.

Reconcile the same purchase dollars once
AllocationIllustrative amountReview question
Land$100,000Which evidence supports the nondepreciable allocation?
Reviewed shorter-lived assets$70,000Which identified assets support each assigned class and basis?
Remaining building basis$330,000Which property classification, method and convention apply?
Total purchase allocation$500,000Does it reconcile to the same transaction?

The review reallocates the $400,000 depreciable amount; it does not add $70,000 to it. Leaving $400,000 as building basis and also depreciating the $70,000 would duplicate purchase dollars. Separately purchased furnishings and later improvements need their own evidence and reconciliation so that an item included in the sale is not counted again from a second inventory. Closing costs and basis adjustments are omitted from this simplified example and require separate review.

Bonus eligibility is another decision

IRS Publication 946 describes the restored 100% special depreciation allowance for certain qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, subject to the applicable rules and elections. It also defines placed in service by readiness and availability for the intended use. A closing date alone does not establish that date or eligibility. Have the preparer review acquisition facts, asset qualification, use, elections and supporting dates.

If the fictional $70,000 allocation is entirely eligible and the applicable allowance is 100%, that allowance would be $70,000 before considering the remaining assets and other tax-return limitations. This is conditional arithmetic. It is not $70,000 of cash received, a statement that every study component qualifies, or a conclusion that the deduction offsets the buyer’s other income this year. The remaining building calculation requires its own classification and timing; this example does not assign every STR the same recovery period.

Prepare a review file before relying on the result

  • Acquisition evidence: retain the contract, amendments and final settlement records. Ask the advisor how the acquisition date is determined under the applicable rules rather than selecting the most convenient document date.
  • Asset identity: reconcile inventories, invoices and photographs to the property and items actually acquired. Mark seller assertions separately from inspected or otherwise supported facts.
  • Readiness timeline: record completed work, furnishing, permissions and availability. Have the advisor assess what establishes the relevant service dates; do not use the first reservation as a substitute for the entire file.
  • Allocation support: request an explanation of land, building and component assignments, source costs, methods, assumptions and exclusions. An unexplained percentage does not answer those questions.
  • Return-level review: ask the tax professional to evaluate basis, at-risk, passive-activity, personal-use and other applicable limitations before estimating a current benefit. Keep the study output and the usable deduction as separate decisions.
  • Future disposition: ask how the schedules, basis adjustments and potential recapture affect a sale model. A large early deduction is not a complete holding-period comparison.

Compare the acquisition before and after the tax review

Create three versions of the purchase decision: operating cash flow with no assumed tax benefit; the advisor-reviewed tax scenario; and a timing downside where an expected current deduction is unavailable or deferred. Record the evidence and unresolved assumptions that differ between them. Keep the property’s operating reserves available for actual bills rather than spending an estimated future tax benefit before the tax review is complete.

For the fictional allocation above, the open-item register might show “allocation reconciled to $500,000,” “component eligibility under review,” and “current-year usability not determined.” Those are useful separate statuses. A completed study or a large number on a worksheet should not turn all three into “approved.” Ask who is responsible for each conclusion and when it is needed for the buyer’s decision.

Continue with the cash-flow and downside example and ownership and closing review. Discuss the actual facts with a qualified tax advisor; AE Tax Advisors’ real-estate planning resources can help prepare questions.
