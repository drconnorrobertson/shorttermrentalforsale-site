Short-Term Rental Property Evidence Checklist

A property listing can give you a starting point, but the acquisition file needs evidence tied to the exact address. Use this checklist before relying on a seller's description of an operating short-term rental. It organizes questions for your broker, lender, insurer and local permitting office; it does not establish that a property is approved for your proposed use.

Build an address and identity record first

Record the street address, parcel number, seller name, listing contact and source of each fact. Check the applicable public property records and ask your closing professional to investigate inconsistencies between the advertised owner and the proposed seller. A copied photo or a working door code is insufficient evidence of authority to transact.

The FTC explains how copied rental listings and substituted contact details can mislead renters. Its guidance concerns rental scams, rather than a title examination for a purchase. For a purchase, use the warning as a reason to verify contact details independently and have your closing team review ownership and signing authority.

Ask the jurisdiction an address-specific question

Write down the proposed operation: rental duration, guest capacity, owner occupancy, parking and any planned alterations. Send those facts to the relevant local office and retain its response, date and cited rule. Ask whether a permit is required, whether an existing permit can transfer, and which inspections or renewal steps apply. A neighbor's listing and a seller's permit number do not answer whether your own proposed operation is allowed.

Keep association restrictions in a separate row. Obtain current governing documents and amendments from the appropriate source. Have a qualified local adviser resolve conflicts between public requirements, deed restrictions and association rules rather than combining them into one unsupported “legal” checkbox.

Connect hazard research to an actual insurance quote

Save the property address and map information you reviewed, including the date. The USGS identifies FEMA as the official public source for flood maps used for insurance purposes. A map review is one input; ask the insurer to explain the coverage, exclusions, deductibles and intended rental use for this property. Keep the written quote with the evidence file and ask which assumptions could change before binding coverage.

Separate the asset from the business presentation

Create individual rows for furnishings, future reservations, deposits, management contracts, account access, permits and vendor arrangements. For each row record what the seller says is included, what the contract actually transfers, who must consent, and the document that supports the conclusion. Do not assume reviews, listing accounts or a guest calendar follow ownership of the building.

Use the turnkey inventory guide to document physical items and the seller booking revenue guide to reconcile the income presentation separately.

Use a decision log rather than a completion score

Download the property evidence log. Assign one reviewer and one next action to each unresolved item. Use “supported,” “needs clarification” or “unverified” and link the underlying document. A file with nine completed rows and one unresolved operating-permission issue is not automatically ready to close.

Example: the seller supplies a current rental permit bearing the seller's name. Your next action is to request the jurisdiction's answer about transfer and your planned use. Mark the issue unresolved until that answer is documented, even if historical bookings are strong. Ask your advisers which findings affect your contract deadlines and closing decision.
